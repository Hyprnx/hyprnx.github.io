# Abstract

This report comes from a four-day mentor trip to Singapore through the Techcombank AI Accelerator program. The two-day Singapore Tech Week event was my main starting point for learning about AI and data. I began to see them not only as models and applications, but also as part of a larger system involving technology, industry, institutions, and people. That observation started before the conference. From leaving the aircraft to exiting Changi Airport's terminal took only approximately 20 minutes, including immigration through an autogate and collecting checked baggage. I then used Singapore's connected MRT, bus, building, and pedestrian infrastructure. These experiences elevated my viewpoint on how technology creates value when many parts of a customer journey work together.

From this starting point, the report examines the system that makes AI progress possible. Growing demand for AI requires land, public policy, funding, electricity, water, cooling, construction materials, semiconductor supply, networks, software, skills, governance, and trust. A model may look weightless inside an application, but it depends on a large physical and institutional system. Research from the International Energy Agency, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Nature journals, UNITAR/ITU, the World Bank, OECD, and Singapore-specific studies extends the observations from the event.

The report then compares Singapore's visible infrastructure and operating capability with Vietnam's history and development path. It considers how Singapore connected infrastructure, institutions, transport, logistics, investment, skills, and public services. It also considers how Vietnam's larger size and different history create both coordination differences and opportunities. The aim is not to copy Singapore. Gia Binh International Airport and preparations for APEC 2027 provide a concrete case for asking whether a major Vietnamese project can combine strong construction with coordinated operations, good customer journeys, digital systems, and long-term public value.

The final section returns to Techcombank. It considers how the bank might contribute through its own customer journeys, responsible use of AI and data, supply-chain finance, infrastructure and transition finance, digital services, talent, standards, and partnerships. The report's main observation is that technological progress does not come from models alone. Long-term value depends on whether organizations and countries can build resilient, sustainable, skilled, and trusted systems around AI and data, then turn global progress into useful results for institutions and society.

# Introduction

## Context and motivation

An AI service used in real work is more than a model. It needs data, computing power, memory, networks, storage, software, user identity, monitoring, electricity, cooling, buildings, staff, contracts, money, and rules. Generative AI has increased demand for specialized AI chips, fast memory, high-speed networks, liquid cooling, power equipment, and systems that can answer requests quickly. The International Energy Agency estimates that data-center electricity use could rise from about 415 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2024 to about 945 TWh in 2030, with AI as a major reason for the increase [IEA, 2025].

This growth must be understood as a development issue, not only as a technology issue. Where should new facilities be built? Who receives electricity and water when AI demand competes with homes and other industries? Who pays for new power lines and grids? Who has the skills to build and operate these systems? Who controls important minerals and computer chips? Who carries the environmental and stranded-asset risk if a facility is no longer useful? Who receives the productivity benefits?

## Personal observation and questions

The report asks five related questions: What does the author think about AI development after attending Singapore Tech Week? Which material, financial, labor, and geopolitical issues received less attention? How do large-scale conditions and detailed technical systems affect each other? What does Singapore's development path tell us about building national capability? What questions does this create for Vietnam and Techcombank?

# From Singapore journey to Tech Week: the visible operating stack

## Arrival, transport, and the customer journey

My first important observation happened before Singapore Tech Week. It happened during the journey from the aircraft into the city. In many urban journeys I have taken in Vietnam, people allow extra time because traffic, queues, transfers, and administrative steps may be uncertain. This is not unique to Vietnam; many cities have similar conditions. The difference becomes clear when two systems handle the same basic journey in different ways.

Around the world, passengers are often told to arrive at least two hours before boarding. In some situations, people allow four hours. This is understandable. Travelers are trying to protect themselves against uncertain traffic, check-in, security, immigration, gates, and baggage processes. The extra buffer is sometimes treated humorously, but it also shows how people adapt when public systems are unpredictable.

At Singapore Changi International Airport, my experience was very different. From leaving the plane, passing immigration through an automatic gate, collecting my checked luggage, and leaving the terminal, the whole process took about 20 minutes. Immigration itself took about 20 seconds. This was one personal experience, not a controlled benchmark, so it should not be treated as a universal airport statistic. Still, it was a clear example of a complicated chain of steps feeling predictable, short, and easy to follow. Some domestic journeys I have taken in Vietnam have taken longer than this entire arrival experience.

The impressive part was not one fast machine. It was the way the whole journey was coordinated. An automatic immigration gate can save time, but it cannot fix every other weak point, such as poor signs, slow baggage handling, too few staff, or a difficult connection to ground transport. The final experience depends on physical design, procedures, staff, digital identity, information systems, queue management, security, communication, and related services.

This is the first link between my trip and the report's AI and data theme. Technology creates value when it supports a complete customer journey. A model, sensor, database, or automatic gate is only one part. People experience the reliability of the entire chain. The important question is not only whether one task has been automated. It is whether the points where different activities meet have been designed so the whole system is clear, predictable, and able to recover from disruption.

The same impression continued after I left the airport. I could travel around Singapore by public transport. Singapore is a small country, comparable in scale to Hanoi as a Vietnamese urban reference point, yet its MRT network extends for about 240 kilometres [Land Transport Authority of Singapore, n.d.]. Mass Rapid Transit (MRT) stations connected with buildings, shopping malls, walking routes, and underground spaces. In some places, going to a building's basement led directly to an MRT station. Bus stops were spread across the city, and transport was planned as part of the urban environment rather than as a separate service beside it. This reduced the amount of planning I had to do at each destination.

Transport also works as an information system. It needs route planning, schedules, fares, passenger information, station-capacity planning, maintenance, incident management, accessibility, and coordination between physical assets. The visible convenience depends on data and disciplined operations. This is similar to an enterprise AI system: users see an answer or a smooth journey, but many hidden services must work together. My journey is therefore a lens, not a national performance measure. The historical and institutional evidence later in the report helps examine the wider capability behind that impression.

Singapore's efficiency should not be treated as perfection, and Vietnam should not be described as incapable. Singapore has a smaller territory, a concentrated urban form, strong planning institutions, and decades of infrastructure investment. Vietnam has a much larger population, many regions, different administrative conditions, and a different history. The useful comparison is not “Singapore is efficient and Vietnam is inefficient.” It is this: which connections, standards, planning choices, and operating skills make one system feel more reliable than another?

This question also gives the Singapore Tech Week agenda a more personal meaning. The event discussed AI infrastructure, but my first lesson about infrastructure came from immigration, baggage, roads, MRT stations, and walking connections. Before AI can change an organization, organizations and cities need to connect systems around real customer journeys. Data and technology are most valuable when they reduce friction instead of passing the burden on to the user.

## Singapore Tech Week observations

The main pattern in the agenda was a move from AI ambition to the conditions needed to use AI in real organizations. The sessions covered business readiness, cloud and AI infrastructure, important networks, data quality and governance, the cost of answering AI requests, AI factories, electricity, cooling, water, cyber resilience, safe testing areas for AI agents, platform engineering, site reliability engineering (SRE), privacy, safety rules, and responsible AI.

The highlighted Mainstage sessions showed this change in focus. Google DeepMind's work on general-purpose robotics connected AI models with machines that act in the physical world. NVIDIA's discussion of world models and quantum intelligence connected future AI progress with future computing systems. FuriosaAI's session connected efficient AI inference—the process of using a trained model to produce an answer—with power, space, and cost. The session on strategic networks showed that moving data can be as important as processing it. ESA's space-AI discussion showed that sustainability concerns reach beyond one data center.

Other sessions made the large and small parts of the system visible. **Powering the AI Era**, **From Risk to Resilience: Water Sustainability for AI Data Centers**, **Southeast Asia's Water Reality Check**, and **Rising Costs, Limited Energy** connected AI growth with physical resources. **The Next AI Bottleneck is Not Models** and **The Future of AI Demands** pointed to limits before the model itself. **From Compute to Cloud**, **Rethinking the Data Path for AI Inference**, **Maximizing Tokens per Watt**, **Network Automation and Multi-Tenancy**, and **HPE Turnkey Solution for NVIDIA's AI Factory** addressed the design and operation of AI systems. Sessions about agent safety rules, cross-border data protection, trustworthy AI, and responsible AI connected fast deployment with control and accountability.

The agenda was strong on the operating stack but less complete on the wider political and economic system behind it. It did not give one complete treatment of land use, construction materials, electronic waste, mineral extraction, labor conditions, advanced chip packaging, high-bandwidth memory, how capital is distributed, assets that may become unusable, or the US-China technology competition. Sustainability appeared often, but usually through operational efficiency, power, cooling, and water rather than through a full account of impacts across an asset's entire life.

For me, the hardware-focused sessions created an important change in perspective. Discussions about server racks, power generators, cooling systems, networks, and other equipment showed that AI is not only software or a model on a screen. It is also a physical system whose parts must be built, connected, powered, cooled, and maintained. This is true in cloud facilities and in on-premises systems owned and operated by an organization. Understanding how those hardware parts are composed helps explain why AI development begins with a much larger infrastructure story.

# Macro ecosystem: the conditions that make AI possible

## Land, siting, and public legitimacy

A data center begins as a decision about land. Its location affects access to electricity, power lines, internet fiber, water, cooling conditions, workers, customers, roads, and protection from disasters. It also determines which communities experience construction, noise, competition for water, higher land prices, or changes to industrial land.

Cheap land may be far from power grids, internet connections, and skilled workers. An urban site may offer better connections but face limits on land and water and more community concerns. A site near renewable power may still need new transmission lines. Approval should therefore consider reliable power rather than only the amount of electricity that can be generated, competition for water, construction and end-of-life impacts, available workers, community support, and the ability to expand or change the site later.

A Singapore study by Liu et al. describes data centers as a sustainability issue in a country with limited land and water. Singapore stopped approving new data centers in 2019 and later reopened approvals with new requirements and limits [Liu et al., 2025]. Digital infrastructure can create economic value while still competing with homes, factories, water needs, and climate goals.

## Policy and institutional coordination

AI infrastructure crosses many areas of government: energy, land, water, construction, telecommunications, data protection, trade, education, finance, the environment, and security. Policy therefore tests whether institutions can work together. Support for new AI capacity can conflict with limits on the grid, worker shortages, scarce water, or goals for lower lifecycle carbon emissions.

A useful policy framework connects five areas: technology and innovation; policy and regulation; finance; infrastructure and energy; and people and skills [Liu et al., 2025]. Practical measures include predictable permits, public information about grid and water capacity, energy and water reporting, rules for construction and decommissioning, clear data-governance requirements, incentives tied to results, workforce programmes, cybersecurity duties, and community participation.

The goal is not only to build quickly. It is to build capacity that is useful, reliable, and accepted by society, while avoiding costs that could have been prevented.

## Capital and risk

The money needed for AI infrastructure pays for land, design, buildings, substations, transmission lines, generators, uninterruptible power supply (UPS) systems, cooling, fiber, servers, storage, security, insurance, software, maintenance, and replacement. Public utilities may also pay for grid upgrades that serve private data centers.

Funding should be considered in separate groups: money for the main project, shared infrastructure, technology, people and training, upgrades and reuse, and insurance and backup systems. The important questions are who receives the value, who pays for supporting infrastructure, and who carries the loss if demand or use is lower than expected. A facility built too early may become a stranded asset—an asset that cannot earn enough value to justify its cost. Too little capacity can limit productivity and reliability.

The Singapore agenda discussed scale, capital, delivery risk, financing models, and return on investment (ROI). A fuller economic analysis must also ask how gains and risks are shared among facility owners, cloud providers, chip companies, customers, utilities, governments, workers, and communities.

## Electricity and grids

The International Energy Agency estimates that global data-center electricity use could more than double from about 415 TWh in 2024 to 945 TWh in 2030 [IEA, 2025]. This is more than a question of total yearly energy. AI creates large, concentrated, and fast-growing electricity loads. These loads may require substations, transmission lines, equipment that keeps power stable, backup generators, batteries, and long-term supply contracts.

At facility level:

$$
P_{\mathrm{facility}} = P_{\mathrm{IT}} \times \mathrm{PUE}.
$$

Here, $P_{\mathrm{facility}}$ is the total electricity drawn by the data center. $P_{\mathrm{IT}}$ is the electricity used by servers, storage, and network equipment. PUE, or Power Usage Effectiveness, is a multiplier for supporting systems such as cooling, lighting, and power conversion. The relationship means that facility electricity rises when computer use rises or when supporting systems use more electricity. A lower PUE means less overhead for the same computing work, but PUE alone does not show the carbon content of the grid, water use, materials used to build the site, or useful work produced. A fuller assessment asks whether power remains available during peak demand and extreme weather, whether renewable power matches demand at the right times rather than only across a whole year, who pays for transmission, and whether flexible workloads or storage can reduce pressure on the grid.

Vietnam's electricity outlook adds a direct consideration for AI and data centers. Nguyen, Ponomarenko, and Nguyen's 2024 study combines data from EVN, the General Statistics Office, the Ministry of Industry and Trade, the coal sector, and national power plans. It uses four economic-growth scenarios and studies energy security, fair access to energy, and environmental sustainability. It is a strategic scenario study, not a detailed model of power-system reliability [Nguyen et al., 2024].

The study gives a warning for 2030. It projects electricity demand at about 124 GW and planned installed capacity at 125.19 GW. That leaves only 1.19 GW, or roughly 1%, of nominal headroom. This margin can disappear when reserve capacity, outages, fuel disruptions, drought, variable renewable generation, or transmission limits are considered. For comparison, Vietnam had about 80.56 GW of installed generation capacity and 280.63 TWh of electricity production in 2023. The study's 2030 figures are about 125.19 GW of capacity and 529.57 TWh of production. Its projected capacity includes about 76.88 GW of renewables, including hydropower, or 61.41% of total capacity; 30.97 GW of coal, 11.14 GW of gas, 44.40 GW of solar, and 46.62 GW of wind. The authors expect no new coal plants after 2030 and greater use of solar, wind, hydropower, biomass, hydrogen, and storage. Their estimated generation costs range from about USD 0.05/kWh for wind to USD 0.0865--0.0974/kWh for coal, with solar, hydropower, and gas between those values.

This outlook matters for data and AI centers. Cloud services can place some computing and electricity use outside Vietnam, but that choice creates stronger requirements for data protection, cross-border transfer controls, access management, and legal compliance. Building capacity inside Vietnam can support local capability, but it must be planned together with power supply, transmission, cooling, and water.

## Cooling and water

As servers become more powerful and racks become denser, air cooling becomes harder. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling, rear-door heat exchangers, immersion cooling, chilled-water loops, dry coolers, and mixed systems each involve different trade-offs among efficiency, water, cost, maintenance, and risk of failure.

Water has at least three parts:

1. Water used or taken directly for cooling.
2. Water used indirectly to generate the electricity.
3. Water used to make construction materials and computer chips.

$$
W_{\mathrm{total}} = W_{\mathrm{direct}} + W_{\mathrm{electricity}} + W_{\mathrm{embodied}}.
$$

Here, $W_{\mathrm{total}}$ is the report's whole water footprint. $W_{\mathrm{direct}}$ is water used at the facility for cooling. $W_{\mathrm{electricity}}$ is water used elsewhere to produce the facility's electricity. $W_{\mathrm{embodied}}$ is water used to make the building, equipment, and chips. The equation shows why reducing water at the site does not always reduce the total footprint: the water burden may move to electricity generation or manufacturing.

Dry cooling may reduce local water use but require more electricity. Evaporative cooling may reduce electricity use but take more water. The best choice depends on the location. The Singapore research warns that rules often measure visible operating efficiency while overlooking construction, upgrades, and decommissioning [Liu et al., 2025].

## Materials, minerals, and waste

AI infrastructure needs concrete, steel, copper, silicon, chemicals, batteries, circuit boards, memory, cables, and cooling equipment. Making computer chips and building facilities moves some environmental impacts upstream, where they may be less visible to the final user.

UNITAR and ITU report 62 million tonnes of electronic waste worldwide in 2022. They project 82 million tonnes by 2030. Only 22.3% of the 2022 total was formally collected and recycled [UNITAR/ITU, 2024]. AI accelerators may become too old to use profitably before they physically break. This creates a trade-off among efficiency, use, resale, repair, reuse, and waste.

For this report, the full AI footprint can be organized as:

$$
F_{\text{AI}} = F_{\text{embodied}} + F_{\text{construction}} + F_{\text{operation}} + F_{\text{end-of-life}}.
$$

Here, $F_{\text{AI}}$ is the total footprint considered in the report. $F_{\text{embodied}}$ covers impacts created while making materials and equipment. $F_{\text{construction}}$ covers building the facility. $F_{\text{operation}}$ covers impacts while the system is running. $F_{\text{end-of-life}}$ covers disposal, recycling, reuse, or replacement. This is a reporting framework, not a new numerical model. It helps prevent attention from focusing only on electricity used during operation.

The question is not only how efficient a server is. It is also what useful service the entire system provides during its life and what materials and waste it creates.

## Workforce and capability

The ecosystem needs machine-learning researchers, but it also needs electricians, thermal and civil engineers, network and storage specialists, semiconductor engineers, construction managers, site-reliability engineers, security staff, data stewards, compliance specialists, technicians, and people who work in shifts to operate facilities.

There are two broad layers of workers. One layer contains scarce specialists in chip design, power electronics, cooling, distributed systems, and AI operations. The other is much larger: managers and professionals who need to use, understand, govern, and question AI. OECD research says that AI skills are not limited to coding. Fewer than 1% of workers may need advanced AI skills, while many more need digital, data, management, problem-solving, creative, and innovation skills [OECD, 2024]. The World Bank reports strong growth in AI-related job postings in middle-income economies [World Bank, 2025a].

Vietnam therefore needs both advanced researchers and a broad pipeline of technicians, engineers, auditors, subject-matter experts, data-literate managers, and people trained in responsible technology.

From the author's perspective, this workforce question also connects to Techcombank Group's 2020--2025 five-year strategic plan. The Group's existing capabilities could provide a basis for investing in, or partnering on, data-center capacity operated in Vietnam. Such a contribution would require long-term skills, capital, power, cooling, security, and responsible data governance. This is a personal development perspective, not an announced Techcombank commitment.

## Chips, minerals, and geopolitics

An AI accelerator is the final product of a long supply chain. That chain includes chip design, design software, factories, lithography equipment, advanced packaging, high-bandwidth memory, circuit-board materials, networking, cooling, and software. If any one part is limited, the complete AI system is limited. A “GPU shortage” therefore means more than too few GPUs; it can mean a shortage anywhere in this chain.

Export controls can limit access to advanced logic chips, chip-making equipment, and memory. Restrictions on critical minerals can affect gallium, germanium, rare earths, and other inputs. CNAS and CSIS analyses reviewed for this report describe tight AI-chip supply, long factory timelines, limits in advanced packaging and memory, and the connection between semiconductor controls and mineral restrictions [CNAS, n.d.; CSIS, n.d.].

Vietnam, like many countries, depends heavily on NVIDIA and suppliers connected to China for important chips and components. In the author's view, Vietnam may not yet have enough specialized workers and industrial depth to design and manufacture advanced AI accelerators fully in-house. This makes long-term education in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) an important investment. STEM education alone will not create a complete chip industry, but it can build the skills needed for chip design, manufacturing, power systems, cooling, software, and AI operations.

Singapore and Vietnam cannot make every part of the supply chain themselves. They can, however, use several suppliers, build trusted institutions, develop local skills and capabilities, and avoid depending on one supplier or route.

## Sustainability and useful output

Higher efficiency can still lead to higher total demand because cheaper AI makes people use more AI. A new facility may reduce its operating carbon emissions while creating large emissions during construction. A low-carbon power contract may also leave local grid congestion or water stress unresolved.

The 2025 *Nature Sustainability* study estimates annual water footprints of $731$--$1{,}125$ million $\mathrm{m^3}$ and additional carbon emissions of $24$--$44$ Mt CO$_2$-equivalent from US AI-server deployment between 2024 and 2030, depending on scale and assumptions [Xiao et al., 2025]. Better practices can reduce these impacts, but they cannot remove physical limits.

Sustainability should therefore measure useful work, not only efficiency:

$$
E_{\mathrm{useful}} = \frac{\text{useful task output}}{\text{kWh} + \lambda_w\text{water} + \lambda_c\text{carbon} + \lambda_m\text{material burden}}.
$$

Here, $E_{\mathrm{useful}}$ is a conceptual measure of useful output. The numerator is the work the AI system actually delivers. The denominator combines electricity, water, carbon, and material burden. The symbols $\lambda_w$, $\lambda_c$, and $\lambda_m$ represent the importance assigned to those three non-electricity costs in a particular decision. This is not a standard published metric or a claim that all costs can be reduced to one exact number. It is the author's way of showing why tokens per watt alone may hide other costs. The weights will differ by place and by decision.

# Micro architecture: how the system operates

## Facility and electrical path

The technical system begins with a site and building that can support heavy floors, rack layouts, fire protection, security, temperature control, expansion, and changing rack densities. Long-lasting parts of the building should be separated from replaceable computer equipment, so the facility can support several generations of hardware.

Electricity moves through grid connections, substations, medium-voltage distribution, transformers, switchgear, uninterruptible power supply (UPS) systems, power-distribution units, rack connections, voltage controls, and server power supplies. Every conversion loses some energy and creates possible failure points. AI clusters can also create sudden and synchronized loads, so stable power matters. Generators, batteries, UPS systems, and storage connected to the grid differ in response time, operating duration, emissions, maintenance needs, and cost.

## Thermal, network, and data paths

Every joule of electricity used by a computer becomes heat. Air cooling is familiar, but it becomes less effective as equipment gets denser. Liquid cooling can support denser systems, but it adds pumps, pipes, leak detection, coolant-quality checks, maintenance procedures, and new ways to fail. Cooling choices affect electricity use, water use, rack design, labor, capital cost, and uptime.

AI performance also depends on moving data. Training and inference—the process of using a trained model to produce an answer—require communication among accelerators, memory, storage, and other services. Bandwidth, delay, network design, congestion, data location, and storage speed determine whether expensive chips are working or waiting. This explains the conference focus on strategic networks, storage, shared use of systems, inference data paths, and GPU clusters.

The data path is also a governance path. Data must be classified, access must be approved, encryption must be used where needed, records must be kept for the right amount of time, and cross-border transfers must follow the law. A fast system with poor records of where data came from and how it was used is not ready for serious enterprise use.

## Heterogeneous compute and workload economics

Different chips do different jobs. CPUs handle coordination, data preparation, control logic, storage, and general services. GPUs perform many calculations in parallel and have mature software support. TPUs and other application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) can provide better performance per watt for stable and specialized workloads. No chip is best for every task. The choice depends on the model, number precision, quantization, batch size, sequence length, memory speed, communication needs, response time, utilization, software support, and supply risk.

Training a model usually needs a synchronized group of chips and very fast connections. Real-time inference needs low and predictable response time. Batch inference needs high total throughput and flexible scheduling. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) adds search indexes, storage, embeddings, and data rules. Agentic systems add memory, identity, tool use, safe testing areas, and audit records.

A practical request-cost identity is:

$$
C_{\mathrm{request}} = C_{\mathrm{compute}} + C_{\mathrm{memory}} + C_{\mathrm{network}} + C_{\mathrm{storage}} + C_{\mathrm{cooling}} + C_{\mathrm{governance}} + C_{\mathrm{risk}}.
$$

This prevents a benchmark that measures only chip time from being mistaken for the cost of running a real production service.

## Orchestration, guardrails, and operations

A production platform needs a control system. It schedules scarce resources, manages identities, records versions, watches system health, and enforces rules. It may include container management, job scheduling, model registries, data and vector stores, secret management, telemetry, policy engines, and automated deployment.

The system should be able to answer: Which model and data version produced this output? Which user, AI agent, or service accessed this dataset? Can a workload be paused or moved? What happens if a GPU, network path, storage volume, or region fails? How are changes in model quality, cost, delay, and safety monitored? Can an AI agent call only the tools it is allowed to use?

Controls should exist at several points: login and identity checks, permissions, input filtering, retrieval controls, output checks, rate limits, cost limits, human approval, audit logs, data retention, and incident response. Runtime guardrails—rules applied while an AI system is running—cannot replace data minimization, secure design, contracts, training, and clear responsibility.

Vietnam also has a legal framework for protecting personal data. Decree No. 13/2023/ND-CP means that AI guardrails should be designed carefully from the planning stage and checked during implementation. Systems should control what personal data is collected, why it is used, who can access it, how long it is kept, and whether it can cross national borders [Government of Vietnam, 2023].

## Reliability and redundancy

Reliability belongs to the whole system. Extra power does not protect a compromised identity service. Copied storage does not guarantee that a model produces a correct answer. In redundancy language, **N** means the minimum number of units needed to carry the full load. **N+1** means that minimum plus one extra unit, so one unit can fail or be maintained while the system continues operating. **2N** means two complete sets of the required system, often called A and B paths, so one full set can fail or be maintained while the other carries the load [Schneider Electric, n.d.; Vertiv, n.d.]. Possible protections include N+1 or 2N power and cooling, two power paths, spare hardware, copied storage, several availability zones, backup regions, training checkpoints, reduced-service modes, separate control systems, and tested recovery procedures.

Every backup choice costs money, energy, unused capacity, complexity, and labor. A payment service, a public-safety application, a recommendation system, and an experimental training job should not all have the same availability target.

Operational measures should include availability, incidents, response time, throughput, cost per task, chip utilization, PUE, water effectiveness, carbon intensity, network and memory use, model quality, model drift, security violations, recovery time, hardware replacement, and on-call workload. The aim is not to create more dashboards. It is to avoid optimizing only one part of the system.

# Synthesis: the ecosystem flywheel

AI demand creates a feedback loop. New models and applications attract funding. That funding pays for chips, cloud capacity, and facilities. Those facilities need land, electricity, cooling, networks, materials, and workers. More capacity supports business adoption, and adoption creates more data, income, and demand.

The same loop can create limits. Power, water, advanced chip packaging, memory, permits, or skilled operators may grow more slowly than demand for AI. Environmental costs can lead to public opposition or new rules. Export controls can divide supply chains and lengthen delivery times. Better efficiency can also increase total use because lower prices encourage more usage.

The main limit changes from place to place. In one location, land and water may be the main limits. In another, the limits may be transmission lines, funding, skilled workers, or reliable suppliers. The important national question is not only how many GPUs exist. It is how many complete systems are powered, cooled, connected, governed, and operated well enough to deliver useful results.

Three time horizons help organize decisions:

- **Immediate:** keep services reliable, safe, lawful, and affordable.
- **Build:** add facilities, power, networks, skills, and supply capacity without creating avoidable long-term limits.
- **Transform:** build local capability, reuse materials, use cleaner energy, strengthen institutions, and develop higher-value applications.

These time horizons move the question from how an AI system works to how a country builds the abilities needed to operate such systems reliably: coordinated institutions, infrastructure, skills, funding, logistics, and public trust. Singapore is a useful case for studying how those abilities are built.

# Singapore's development path

Singapore started with a small home market, few natural resources, no large hinterland, unemployment, labor unrest, and a strong dependence on trade through its port. Its response was to build an export-focused economy. The government supported this approach with infrastructure, investment attraction, skills, and institutions that could work together [Singapore Government, n.d.; World Bank, 2024b].

Singapore's location became valuable because its port, customs system, logistics, airport, communications, financial services, legal services, and public administration made it easy for companies to use the city as a base. The Maritime and Port Authority describes Singapore as a global hub port that supports many maritime networks and services [MPA, n.d.]. Geography became an advantage because it was turned into working capability.

Specialized public institutions connected investment promotion, industrial land, ports, utilities, housing, transport, education, and finance. This reduced friction for companies and created a connected platform instead of many separate projects. Foreign investment brought money, technology, management methods, access to export markets, quality standards, and demanding customers. The aim was not only to attract money. It was also to create jobs, public revenue, and local capability.

Singapore later moved from labor-intensive industries to petrochemicals, electronics, precision engineering, advanced manufacturing, finance, and knowledge-based services. Rising wages and a shortage of workers pushed the economy to upgrade. Infrastructure also created clusters: shipping attracted finance, insurance, law, warehouses, manufacturers, and headquarters; these businesses then created more demand for connectivity and skills.

Education, training, and lifelong learning were treated as part of the economic system. Finance grew from supporting trade into banking, foreign exchange, insurance, securities, asset management, and regional treasury services. Housing, sanitation, transport, and urban planning helped support workers and gave investors confidence.

The lesson is not to copy a city-state. It is to connect geography, institutions, infrastructure, investment, skills, finance, and continuous improvement. Singapore's current data-center situation also shows that growth has limits when land, water, energy, and the full life of an asset are not included in planning. Connectivity and trust can attract investment, but resource limits require careful choices.

## A comparison of development pathways

The difference between Singapore and Vietnam can be seen in the airport and transport experience, but it has deeper historical causes. Singapore had to make a small, resource-limited city useful to the world. It had little land to absorb waste or delay, no large hinterland to hide weak connections, and a strong need to create jobs and public revenue after independence. These conditions encouraged a coordinated approach: attract investment, build infrastructure, educate workers, connect the port and airport to world trade, and keep upgrading the economy.

Vietnam followed a different path. It is much larger, with a large domestic market, a long coastline, several urban and industrial regions, important agricultural and manufacturing capacity, and large differences in infrastructure and public services between regions. The Doi Moi reforms and later trade agreements moved the country from a centrally planned economy toward a more market-based and globally connected economy. Foreign investment and export manufacturing created jobs, industrial capacity, and trade growth. The World Bank's Vietnam 2045 work says that the next stage needs more value created inside Vietnam, stronger services, more skilled workers, better links with local suppliers, and lower-carbon productivity [World Bank, 2024a].

This is not a ranking of national character. It is a comparison of particular systems. Singapore has advantages in compact urban planning, coordination between institutions, public transport, logistics, and predictable services. Vietnam has advantages in its scale, large working-age population, growing home market, manufacturing experience, coastline, regional connections, and ability to develop several urban and industrial corridors. Vietnam does not need to reproduce Singapore's exact form. It can learn how Singapore connects infrastructure, institutions, talent, funding, and user experience, then apply those principles at Vietnamese scale.

Four dimensions are especially useful. **Coordination time** means how quickly a decision moves from policy to land, construction, utilities, staff, and operation. **Interface quality** means whether a passenger, citizen, business, or bank customer experiences one connected journey or must solve the gaps between agencies. **Domestic value capture** means whether investment creates Vietnamese suppliers, engineering skills, services, research, and management expertise. **Measurement** means whether institutions track not only whether a project was completed, but also waiting time, reliability, accessibility, lifecycle cost, resource use, and trust.

Vietnam can improve without choosing between fast construction and careful planning. The goal is not to remove every queue or guarantee that every project works perfectly. The goal is to make delays visible, responsibilities clear, data appropriately shared, and improvement continuous. A larger country can use several hubs, shared standards, regional specialization, and digital coordination to build capabilities that a compact city-state cannot easily reproduce.

This view changes how technological development should be understood. AI adoption in a bank, airport, city, or industrial zone will not succeed simply because an organization buys a model or platform. It will succeed when physical processes, data, people, governance, and incentives are arranged around a useful service. Singapore's visible efficiency is therefore a lesson about integration, not only a reason to buy more automation.

## Gia Binh as a test of integrated infrastructure

The airport comparison creates a concrete question for Vietnam. Public project materials reviewed for this report associate Masterise Aviation Infrastructure (MAI) with the Gia Binh project, which is being developed in connection with Vietnam's preparations for APEC 2027. The project website presents Gia Binh as a large aviation and logistics development. daa International describes work on airport design review, readiness for operations, and a VIP terminal intended for the APEC timetable [Gia Binh International Airport, n.d.; daa International, n.d.]. The exact corporate and approval structure should be checked against official disclosures as the project develops.

The airport is still being developed, so this report does not judge its operating performance. Instead, it suggests standards that could be used to judge it in the future.

Whether Gia Binh can match the efficiency I experienced at Changi should not be judged by the building's appearance or the number of runways. It should be judged by the complete passenger and logistics journey. Useful measures could include the time from aircraft door to public exit, immigration time, baggage delivery and delays, signs and navigation, accessibility, road and public-transport connections, transfer reliability, cargo clearance, staff response, recovery from disruption, data protection, and the experience of visitors who do not know the airport.

APEC 2027 is an early milestone for VIP access and event readiness. The official project material presents a roadmap extending to 2050 [Gia Binh International Airport, n.d.]. The 2027 milestone can create a clear near-term service standard, but the airport's everyday operating model, maintenance, staffing, connections with other transport, and long-term customer-data systems must continue developing after the event. Readiness for an event and durable public infrastructure are related but not identical. The airport must work after 2027 for ordinary passengers, workers, logistics companies, families, and businesses.

The Changi lesson is not that Vietnam must copy one airport. It is that airport performance depends on many parts working together. Immigration, baggage, security, airlines, transport, retail, construction, digital identity, passenger information, and emergency response need shared standards and connections. If each organization improves only its own part, the passenger still experiences the total delay. If the journey is designed as one system, improvements in one part can support the others.

This matters to Techcombank because a financial institution understands journeys, transactions, identity, trust, risk, and reliable service. It may not operate an airport, but it can contribute through funding, payment systems, customer-data governance, analytics, supply-chain finance, project controls, and digital services. Its role should remain clear: it will not replace airport operators, public authorities, engineers, transport planners, or airlines. But it can help make the financial and information parts of a large infrastructure system more reliable and measurable.

The broader question is not simply “Can Gia Binh be as efficient as Changi?” It is: can Vietnam use a major project to build repeatable skills in integrated planning, digital operations, customer-journey design, transparent measurement, and domestic supplier development?" If so, the airport could become more than a transport asset. It could the very first brick that construct the Aerotropolis like it's promises, which later support logistics, tourism, trade, technology, skills, and regional development.

# Implications for Vietnam and Techcombank

## Vietnam's next transition

Vietnam has gained from trade and foreign direct investment (FDI). However, the World Bank's *Viet Nam 2045: Trading Up in a Changing World* says that export-led growth alone will not be enough for Vietnam to become a high-income country [World Bank, 2024a]. The report notes that foreign companies produce a large share of exports, domestic participation in global value chains fell from 35% in 2009 to 18% in 2023, services make up about 12% of exports, only about 5% of manufacturing workers are highly skilled, and university participation remains limited.

These figures point to areas where Vietnam can improve. The next gains can come from higher productivity, stronger local suppliers, deeper services, better skills, stronger institutions, long-term funding, and lower-carbon production. Singapore offers lessons about coordination and capability building. It does not offer a model that Vietnam should copy exactly.

## Techcombank contribution

The below statements are author's personal reflection angles

The airport and transport experience suggests three levels at which Techcombank's contribution could be considered. First, the bank can improve its own customer journeys by reducing unnecessary waiting, connecting channels, making decisions more predictable, and using data responsibly to find where customers face difficulty. Second, it can help business customers improve their journeys through payments, working capital, trade finance, digital identity, risk tools, and supply-chain connections. Third, it can support the wider capability system through infrastructure finance, green and transition finance, talent development, standards, and partnerships.

Fortunately, this reflection does not begin from zero. In the author's view, the Techcombank/Techcom Group data platform already supports decision-making, risk management, and service-level agreement (SLA) monitoring, and it continues to develop. The author's informal comparison is that the platform may be at least five years ahead of some competitors; this is a personal assessment, not an independently verified benchmark. Its existing foundation can support deeper AI integration at the core of banking operations and future consideration of Vietnam-operated data-center capacity, consistent with the Group's 2020--2025 strategic direction.

This three-level view keeps AI connected to practical value. A model that automates a task but leaves the customer confused has limited value. A data platform that does not improve decisions, control risk, or reduce time has not yet created real capability. Useful measures include processing time, errors and repeated work, accessibility, reliability, customer trust, employee workload, cost, security, and results for businesses and households.

**Supply-chain finance and domestic value.** The World Bank identifies supplier development and broad supply-chain finance as priorities. Techcombank could be studied as a possible connector of transaction data, working capital, guarantees, trade finance, credit assessment, and supplier capability. The question is whether finance helps Vietnamese firms become stronger suppliers and exporters, rather than only financing transactions after value has already been created elsewhere.

**Long-term capital.** Infrastructure, housing, logistics, energy, manufacturing, digital systems, and climate resilience need funding with different time horizons and levels of risk. A bank can contribute through project finance, structured finance, syndication, risk management, and partnerships while respecting safety limits. The question is how savings can support productive capacity without creating too much concentration or a mismatch between short-term funding and long-term projects.

**Green and transition finance.** AI and digital infrastructure are not automatically environmentally friendly. Techcombank's sustainability materials describe green-finance activity and a Green Bond Framework [Techcombank, n.d.]. A stronger question is whether finance can reward verified improvements across an asset's life, including upgrades and efficient operations, rather than only new projects with attractive labels.

**Responsible data and AI.** Techcombank's annual-report material describes data and analytics, machine learning, generative AI, and large language models [Techcombank, 2023]. The bank could become a Vietnamese example of using AI where data quality, explainability, security, privacy, model risk, and customer protection matter. The value should be measured through productivity and trust, not only by claiming that AI has been adopted.

**Financial inclusion.** Digital finance can make services cheaper and easier for households and small firms to access. It can also create risks involving fraud, privacy, exclusion, and excessive debt. The goal should be safe and useful access to formal financial services, with clear information and human support.

**Talent and standards.** A major bank connects regulators, technology companies, universities, merchants, exporters, households, and infrastructure providers. It could support practical training, internships, cybersecurity, data-governance practice, consumer protection, and responsible-AI standards. A bank cannot replace national decisions about power, land, education, water, semiconductors, or regulation but it can contribute as one coordinated participant.

Useful future measures include the share of exports created domestically, participation of local suppliers, small and medium-sized enterprise access to supply-chain finance, productivity of financed firms, high-skilled jobs, research turned into products, lifecycle emissions and water use, verified green-finance results, AI incidents, auditability, affordability, and customer trust.

# Implications and unresolved decisions

The evidence changes what should be counted when deciding whether to invest in AI infrastructure. Local electricity and water conditions, advanced chip packaging and memory, building materials, worker availability, funding duration, geopolitical risk, and plans for old equipment can affect the value of a facility or service as much as a performance benchmark. Event evidence helps identify important topics, but investment decisions also need local data about the full life of the project and its ability to handle disruption.

For Vietnam, the immediate considerations are the time needed to coordinate decisions, shared data standards, project measurement, skilled operators, and dependable customer journeys. The longer-term considerations are stronger local suppliers, long-term funding, power and transport capacity, research that becomes useful products, and institutions that remain effective over time. Singapore offers principles of coordination and upgrading, not a template to reproduce.

For Techcombank, an important distinction is between activity and capability. Activity can be measured by funded amounts, deployed models, or completed projects. Capability should also be measured through customer-journey time, repeated work, value created by local suppliers, productivity of financed firms, resource use across an asset's life, AI incidents, auditability, affordability, and customer trust. These measures create a practical basis for future evaluation while leaving ownership and public responsibilities with the proper actors.

# Quantum computing: A distant future

During Techweek, a few Google representatives also shared the current state of quantum computing. That discussion provides a bridge to its possible implications for Techcombank in the distant future. Quantum computing is a possible future capability for a smaller group of difficult tasks. In a distance future, quantum computers would work alongside classical computers to further advance human productivity.

## Why Techcombank should watch it

The most relevant near-term reason for Techcombank to follow quantum computing is cybersecurity. Some widely used advanced encryption methods may be weakened by future quantum machines. Replacing encryption across Techcombank takes time, so it can begin identifying where this protection is used, which information must remain private for many years, and how systems could move gradually to quantum-safe encryption. This preparation is useful even before a practical large-scale quantum computer exists [Scholten et al., 2024].

Other possible finance uses are still research topics:

- **Portfolio and asset allocation:** compare many combinations of investments while balancing return, risk, limits, and customer objectives.
- **Treasury and liquidity planning:** examine funding, cash, collateral, and payment-routing choices under changing conditions.
- **Risk and scenario analysis:** explore a large number of possible market, credit, or operational situations more efficiently if future quantum methods prove useful.
- **Transaction networks and fraud analysis:** study complex relationships between accounts and transactions, although evidence for a practical quantum advantage remains early.

These are possible use cases, not promises. Most banking transactions, AI services, risk controls, and customer applications will continue to run on ordinary computers. A quantum service, if it becomes valuable, may handle one specialized part of a larger workflow while ordinary systems prepare the data, check the result, and serve the customer. Any future pilot should therefore be compared with strong classical methods for cost, speed, accuracy, explainability, security, and operational reliability.

Quantum computing may also support work outside finance. Researchers are exploring possible uses in medicine, chemicals, batteries, other materials, logistics, energy systems, and scientific discovery. These areas provide useful context, but they are not the main focus of this report.

## Three names to know

**IBM Qiskit.** Qiskit is IBM's software toolkit and cloud environment for learning about quantum computing and running experiments. It lets students, researchers, and companies explore the field without owning specialized hardware.

**Google Quantum AI Willow.** Willow is part of Google's research into making quantum computations more reliable. It is a research milestone, not a general-purpose commercial computer. [Google Quantum AI, 2024; Google Quantum AI and Collaborators, 2025].

**Microsoft Majorana 1.** Majorana 1 is Microsoft's proposed hardware route toward more stable quantum computing. The company describes a long-term scale-up path, while related scientific discussion continues to examine what has been independently demonstrated [Microsoft Azure Quantum, 2025; Aghaee et al., 2025; Garisto, 2025]. It should therefore be understood as an introduction to an active research direction, not as a ready-made business capability.

## Practical preparation for Vietnam and Techcombank

Vietnam does not need to invest immediately in building a quantum computer. A sensible first step is to build skills in software, cybersecurity, mathematics, science, and cloud-based experimentation. Universities and companies can learn through small demonstrations and partnerships, while continuing to use classical systems for real operations.

For Techcombank, practical preparation can follow three steps: review long-lived encrypted data and system dependencies; build the ability to replace cryptographic methods without rebuilding every application; and monitor finance use cases such as portfolio design, risk simulation, liquidity planning, and transaction analysis. Education, trusted data, and partnerships may create more value now than buying quantum hardware.

Quantum computing is therefore a future capability to watch. The useful response is neither to ignore it nor to treat company announcements as ready products. It is to understand the possible use cases, protect long-lived information, follow independently checked research, and develop people who can judge when the technology is mature enough to create real value.

# Conclusion

First, Singapore Tech Week 2026 showed that using AI requires a complete operating system. It needs business readiness, data, cloud services, networks, inference, AI factories, electricity, cooling, water, cybersecurity, safety rules, trust, and backup plans. The wider research adds layers that were less visible in the event agenda: land, construction, materials, waste, workers, funding, chip supply, geopolitics, and public acceptance.

Second, the large and small parts of the system cannot be separated. A model needs an accelerator. The accelerator needs memory, packaging, electricity, cooling, and software. The facility needs land, grid capacity, water, and skilled operators. The service needs data rules, identity checks, monitoring, and customer trust. This is why AI and data development should be judged as the building of capability, not only as the purchase of a model.

Third, the Changi and MRT observations provide the report's practical test: infrastructure creates value when the connections between different services create one reliable customer journey. Singapore's history shows how geography became connectivity, connectivity became investment, investment became industrial and human capability, and capability became higher-value services. Vietnam should not copy Singapore mechanically. It can adapt the principles of coordination, measurement, local value creation, and continuous improvement. Gia Binh and APEC 2027 offer a concrete opportunity to test these principles through airport operations, logistics, digital systems, and long-term public value, although the airport's operating performance cannot yet be assessed.

Finally, Techcombank's role should support the wider system, not replace the actors responsible for it. The bank could contribute through supply-chain finance, long-term and transition funding, responsible AI, inclusion, talent, standards, and trusted data. Airports, power, education, transport, and public administration still require many other coordinated actors. The important measure is not activity alone, but whether these contributions improve customer journeys, local capability, productivity, reliability, and trust.

> **The future of AI and data will be determined not only by who builds the best model, but by who coordinates the most resilient, sustainable, capable, and trusted ecosystem around it.**
